Structural and decorative graphics. All strokes and fills reference CSS variables. Every asset traces a specific moment in computing or engineering history — the visual vocabulary is earned, not chosen for aesthetics alone.

01

Dot Grid

Usage

Hero section background layer at 60% opacity beneath the headline group. Subtle texture on the footer surface. Never overlaid on body copy.

fill: var(--c-hairline) · r: 1px · pitch: 24px

Heritage

References printed graph paper used in electronics labs from the 1950s through the 1980s — the physical substrate where engineers sketched circuit diagrams and logic gate layouts. IBM distributed dot-grid notepads to engineering staff. The pattern signals methodical, physical work rather than decorative surface.

02

Punch-Card Motif

Usage

Decorative strip in the hero section at low opacity. Can divide hero from problem section. The corner cut is structurally significant — preserve it.

stroke: var(--c-hairline) · hole rx: 1px · 32 sprocket holes

Heritage

The IBM 80-column punch card (1928–1980s) was the primary computing storage medium. The corner cut was a physical orientation marker — operators could detect an upside-down card instantly. Hole patterns encoded machine instructions in Hollerith code: presence or absence of a hole represented a binary digit. Using this motif directly connects Japolic's routing (present/absent rule matches) to the earliest form of programmatic control.

03

Engineering Registration Marks

Usage

Corner marks frame full-bleed SVG diagram panels. The center crosshair appears on the server-rack diagram. Applied as overlay positioned elements at the four corners of any technical diagram container.

outer: var(--c-hairline) · cross: var(--c-ink) 0.6 opacity · 1px stroke

Heritage

Registration marks have been standard in engineering drawing since the 19th century (ANSI/ISO drafting standards). They appear on multi-layer technical prints to ensure correct layer-to-layer alignment. In early computing, they framed system schematics, timing diagrams, and data-flow charts in mainframe documentation. Using them as UI framing makes the screen read as a technical drawing — authored to a specification, not designed for visual appeal.

04

Isometric Server Rack

Usage

Primary hero diagram. U1 (amber) is the Japolic routing plane — visually positioned above all application services. The amber path shows a request entering, being evaluated, and forwarded to the correct service. Placed in the 5-column right column of the hero.

primary: rgba(244,238,225) · accent: var(--c-amber) · bg: var(--c-ink)

Heritage

The 19-inch rack standard (EIA-310, established 1922, adopted for computing in the 1960s) is the universal unit of physical infrastructure. Isometric projection — parallel projection at 30 degrees — was the engineering illustration standard before 3D CAD. IBM and DEC used isometric line art throughout system manuals from the 1960s–1980s. This style communicates "infrastructure as a designed, physical object" with the honesty of a technical drawing, not the artifice of a marketing render.

System rules

24×24 viewBox. All strokes 1.5px, linecap round. Ink on light surface, paper on dark. Amber accent appears in policy, latency, and mesh only — on the single element that represents the Japolic layer. Scale to 20×20 on mobile, 24×24 on desktop.

stroke-width: 1.5 · stroke-linecap: round · no fill

Heritage

The 1.5px stroke weight references the 0.35mm Rapidograph technical pen — the standard instrument for engineering illustration before CAD. Consistent stroke weight across all icons creates a system that reads as produced to a specification. Round line caps prevent the machine-cut harshness of square caps while maintaining geometric precision. This is the weight that appears in Bell Labs schematic drawings and IBM system manuals from 1960–1985.
